Chapter 3: Chestnut Blight & Memory

Page 12

First there was nothing. Then there were trees. Then a giant chestnut branch grew in the night over the wooden roof of the barn, shielding three generations of Hoels from hail and dry plains storms. They took photographs of it every month with the ancient bellows camera, recording the unhurried limb spreading millimeter by millimeter against the Iowa sky.

For eighty years the tree held steady while the family survived blizzards, mortgages, and European wars. The trunk thickened until three men holding hands could not bridge its circumference.

By midsummer the leaves turned glossy emerald, whispering in winds that traveled unbroken all the way from the Rockies. Speaking "A tree is a passage between earth and sky, an engine running on sunlight and memory that measures time in rings rather than ticking seconds." Nicholas stood beneath the canopy and pressed his palm against the deeply fissured bark. The damp wood was warm to the touch, thrumming faintly with liters of mineral water rising toward the sun. He felt the vibration deep in the base of his skull, an ancient low-frequency song older than vowels.

Somewhere far to the east, microscopic spores had already drifted ashore from Chinese nursery stock, landing on the port docks of Brooklyn. The blight had begun its quiet, unstoppable march westward across four billion trees, but here, on this quiet afternoon in Solon, the chestnut leaves still drank the light without fear.

His grandfather looked down from the porch, wiping his calloused hands on an oily rag. "Listen to her, Nick," he called out across the yard. "You can hear her breathing if the wind drops just so."

And Nicholas listened, not knowing that by the time he grew into a man with cameras of his own, the vast continental forest would be reduced to stumps, charcoal, and black-and-white silver gelatin prints preserved in metal tin boxes.
